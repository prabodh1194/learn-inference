# deepseek-v3-5m6-bite
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aYIPTix80Pg

Let's break down the core idea behind
this paper, the DeepSeek V3 technical
report from DeepSeek AI.
Look at these benchmark bars: math,
code, reasoning, general knowledge. This
open model, DeepSeek V3, goes toe-to-toe
with GPT 4.0 and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, the
best closed models money could buy. Now,
the part that made everyone sit up, they
trained it for about 5 and 1/2 million
dollars, not the hundreds of millions
you'd guess for a model this size. 671
billion parameters, train start to
finish, and remarkably, not a single
loss spike, not one rollback the whole
way. This video is about the stack of
tricks that made a frontier model that
cheap and that stable.
The first reason it's cheap is that most
of the model is asleep at any moment.
DeepSeek V3 is a mixture of experts.
Those 671 billion parameters are split
into a big crowd of specialist
sub-networks, experts. For each token, a
little router picks only a handful of
them to actually run, about 37 billion
parameters worth. So, you get the
knowledge of a giant model, but you only
pay to run a small one on every token. A
shared expert always fires for the
common stuff. The routed experts handle
the specialties. Cheap to train, cheap
to serve. But, this trick has a nasty
failure mode.
Here's the failure mode. If the router
is left alone, it plays favorites. A few
popular experts get swamped with almost
every token while the rest sit idle.
That's called routing collapse, and it
wastes most of your model and jams up
the hardware. The classic fix is to add
an extra penalty to the loss, an
auxiliary loss that punishes imbalance
and forces the router to spread tokens
out. It works, but it's a bully. That
extra loss fights the model's real job
of predicting the next token, and
quality quietly suffers. DeepSeek V3's
fix is wonderfully sneaky. Drop the
auxiliary loss entirely. Instead, give
each expert a little bias dial that's
added to its routing score, but only for
deciding who gets picked, never touching
the weights or the gradients. Then run
it like a thermostat. After every
training step, check each expert's load.
If one is overloaded, nudge its dial
down a hair. If one is starving, nudge
it up. Overloaded experts quietly become
less attractive, idle ones more so, and
the traffic evens out, all without ever
poisoning the learning signal. Balance,
for free.
The second big saving is raw numerical
precision. Normally, you train these
models in 16-bit numbers. DeepSeek V3
does most of the heavy matrix multiplies
in 8-bit floating point, literally half
the bits. That halves the memory and
roughly doubles the math throughput. The
catch is that 8-bits is a tiny window.
Numbers that are too big overflow.
Numbers too small vanish to zero. And a
single outlier value can wreck a whole
block. Their answer is fine-grain
scaling. Instead of one scale factor for
a giant tensor, they chop it into small
tiles and blocks, and give each its own
scale. So every little group of numbers
sits right in the sweet spot of the
8-bit range. The running sums are
accumulated back up in full precision,
and the few most delicate parts of the
network are simply kept in higher
precision. The result was a first. They
showed that FP8 training actually works
stably on a model this enormous, where
everyone else had been too nervous to
try.
Two more speedups round it out. One,
instead of only ever predicting the very
next token, they train the model to also
predict the token after that, with small
extra prediction heads chained on.
Guessing further ahead is a harder,
richer exercise. It rings more learning
out of every token of data, and as a
bonus, those extra heads let the
finished model draft several tokens at
once and run faster. Two, in a mixture
of experts spread across many GPUs,
tokens are constantly shipped between
chips to reach their chosen experts, and
normally the GPUs sit idle waiting for
that shipping. They designed the
pipeline so the communication and the
computation happen at the same time,
hiding almost all of that waiting. Fewer
idle chips, lower cost.
The two headline ideas, in their words.
If a frontier model trained for pocket
change is the kind of thing that makes
your day, like, subscribe, and send it
to the friends still quoting hundred
million-dollar training runs.
Add it all up, and the numbers are
staggering. 14.8 trillion tokens of
training. 2.8 million GPU hours total.
Around five and a half million dollars.
An order of magnitude below what a model
this capable usually costs. The
strongest open model of its moment,
matching the top closed models on
knowledge, math, and code. And the whole
marathon ran without a single
unrecoverable loss spike or rollback,
which, if you've ever baby sat a giant
training run, is almost the most
impressive number on the page. They even
distilled reasoning skill from their own
R1 model into it afterward.
The cost in one line.
So, the recipe for a frontier model on a
budget starts sparse. A mixture of
experts where a router wakes only a
sliver of the network per token. Keep
those experts balanced with bias dials
nudged like thermostats, so you never
pay the auxiliary loss tax. Train in
eight-bit precision with per-tile
scaling to halve the cost without the
numbers blowing up. Predict more than
one token ahead to ring extra signal
from your data and overlap the
cross-ship chatter with real work so no
GPU sits idle. Frontier quality, a tenth
of the bill, and not a single rollback.
The one-line version, DeepSeek V3 is a
600 billion parameter mixture of experts
that reaches closed model quality for a
few million dollars by being sparse,
balanced without a penalty, trained in
eight bits, and never wasting a GPU.
What I love here is that there's no
single silver bullet. It's a dozen
careful engineering decisions, each
shaving off a bit of cost, stacked until
a frontier model fits a startup's
budget. The big labs treat that compute
bill as a moat. Papers like this one
keep quietly showing the moat is mostly
cleverness you can copy. That's my read.
Tell me yours. Until next time.
