# moe-40-percent-trick-bite
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vai47j7ovys

Let's break down the core idea behind
this paper. DeepSeek MOE, DeepSeek's
blueprint for experts that actually
specialize.
Here's a 16 billion parameter model
keeping pace with a 7 billion Lama while
doing about 40% of the math. The trick
isn't a bigger brain. It's carving that
brain into experts that each truly know
their one thing. Let's see how.
Normal mixture of experts looks clever.
Instead of one dense layer, a room of
experts, and each token wakes just a
couple. But with only a handful of big
experts, two bad habits creep in. One
expert gets handed poetry and Python and
biology, a jack of all trades, master of
none. That's knowledge hybridity. And
because every expert sees the boring
common stuff, they all waste parameters
relearning it. That's redundancy. Your
experts aren't experts.
DeepSeek's first move, stop using big
experts. Slice each one in two, say four
thin slivers. And to keep the compute
bill identical, wake four times as many.
Same parameters, same cost. But look
what happens to the choices. Pick two
out of 16, and there are 120 ways to do
it. Slice to 64 and pick eight, and
there are 4.4 billion. Knowledge can now
split into exactly the right narrow
bins. And the finer you slice, the
further this scaling axis pushes.
The number's real, straight from the
paper.
Second move, and it's the elegant one.
Peel off a couple of experts and leave
them always on for every token. Let
those soak up the common everyday
knowledge. Now the routed experts don't
have to keep a copy of it. They're free
to go deep on the rare, distinctive
stuff. Shared experts hold the basics.
Specialists hold the edge.
One risk with letting the router learn
freely, it plays favorites, always
calling the same few experts while the
rest starve. So DeepSeek adds a gentle
balance penalty. Nudge the load even
across experts and across GPUs. Just
enough that everyone gets trained, not
so much that it overrides good routing.
If watching a model get carved into
specialists is your kind of thing, like,
subscribe, and pass it to the friend who
thinks scaling is only ever make it
bigger.
Does it actually work? Here's the tell.
Switch off a model's top routed and
watch the score fall. DeepSeek MoE face
plants far harder than a conventional
MoE, which sounds bad, but means the
opposite. Its experts aren't
interchangeable. Each is carrying
knowledge no one else has. And the
shared expert? You can't remove it at
all. The specialization is real.
So the latter, 2 billion parameters
matches a GShard nearly half again its
size and brushes the dense ceiling, the
theoretical best a MoE can hope for.
Scale to 16 billion and you get LLaMA, 2
7 billion quality for about 40% of the
compute. Push a preliminary 145 billion
and it rivals a dense 67 billion model
at under a third of the cost.
In their words,
the whole recipe. Take a room of big
generalist experts, slice them into fine
slivers so knowledge sorts precisely.
Set a few aside as always on for the
common stuff. Keep the load balanced and
you get experts that genuinely
specialize at a fraction of the compute.
One line. Make the experts small and
many and give the basics their own room.
So every parameter is either a
specialist or a shared foundation, never
a wasted generalist.
Which leaves a question worth sitting
with. If specialization keeps paying off
the finer you slice, where does it stop?
One neuron per expert? That's my read.
Tell me yours. Until next time.
