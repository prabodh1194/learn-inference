# deepseek-v3-engineering
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P9hHsrBoHEg

Let's break down the core idea behind
this paper, the DeepSeek V3 technical
report from DeepSeek AI. We'll walk
through four engineering tricks that
made a frontier class model absurdly
cheap to train.
Start with the number that broke
everyone's brain. DeepSeek V3 is a 671
billion parameter model that trades
blows with GPT-4.0
and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, the best closed
models on the planet. And it was trained
start to finish for about 5 and 1/2
million dollars. For a model this size,
that's roughly an order of magnitude
below what you'd expect. And here's the
part that keeps engineers up at night.
Across the entire run on 14.8 trillion
tokens, they never hit a single
unrecoverable loss spike. No rollbacks.
It just trained. The backbone isn't
exotic. It's two ideas from DeepSeek V2,
multi-head latent attention, which
crushes the memory cost of the KV cache,
and DeepSeek MoE, a mixture of experts
where only 37 billion of those
parameters fire per token. But the
backbone isn't why it's cheap. The
cheapness comes from four engineering
tricks stacked on top. And in this
video, we're going to rebuild all four
from scratch. Balancing the experts with
no loss function at all, predicting
several tokens at once, training the
whole thing in 8-bit floats, and hiding
the network traffic completely
underneath the math. Let's go.
Quick tour of the backbone, because the
tricks bolt onto it. Attention is
multi-head latent attention, worth a
full breakdown on its own, but the
one-liner. Instead of caching a fat
key-value vector per token, it caches a
small compressed latent and rebuilds the
rest on the fly, so long context
inference stops drowning in memory. The
feed-forward layers are DeepSeek MoE,
not one big dense network, but a crowd
of small experts plus a couple of shared
ones that always run. A router reads
each token and wakes only a handful of
specialists. 671 billion parameters live
in the model. Just 37 billion fire for
any given token. Giant brain, small
electricity bill. And that routing is
where our first trick lives.
Here's the failure mode built into every
mixture of experts. The router learns
which experts to trust and left alone,
it plays favorites hard. A few lucky
experts get picked for almost every
token. The rest sit idle, barely
training, wasting the parameters you
paid for. That's routing collapse, and
it's not just wasteful. When your
experts are scattered across dozens of
GPUs, a few overloaded experts means a
few overloaded GPUs, and everyone else
stalls waiting on them. So, your
expensive cluster runs at a fraction of
its speed. The textbook fix is an
auxiliary loss. You add an extra penalty
term that measures how lopsided the
routing is and punishes imbalance,
forcing the router to spread tokens more
evenly. And it works, but it's a bully.
That penalty is fighting the model's
actual job, which is predicting the next
token well. Every gradient step becomes
a tug-of-war. Language quality pulling
one way, the balance penalty pulling the
other. Turn the penalty up and load
evens out, but quality quietly erodes.
Turn it down and quality recovers, but
the experts collapse again. You're stuck
trading one against the other. The whole
game is, can we get balance without
paying that tax?
Deep Seek's answer is elegant because it
refuses to touch the loss function at
all. Give every expert one extra number,
a bias, BI. And here's the surgical
part. You add that bias to the affinity
score only when deciding who gets
selected. Top K routing now picks
experts by score plus bias, SIT plus BI.
But, this is the whole trick. The gate
weight that actually multiplies the
experts output still uses the raw,
original affinity untouched. The bias
steers who gets chosen. It never
distorts how much their answer counts.
Zero fingerprints on the model's math.
So, how do you set the biases? Not with
gradients, with a thermostat. After
every step, you check how many tokens
each expert got. Overloaded? Nudge its
bias down by a tiny amount, gamma.
Starved? Nudge it up by gamma. Written
out, BI becomes BI minus gamma times the
sign of load minus target. Just the
sign, plus or minus one, never a
gradient. An overloaded expert slowly
turns less attractive. A starving one
turns more attractive until a token that
would have been ignored crosses the line
and lands back in its top K. And because
it's a side control loop, not a loss
term, it never pulls against next token
prediction. Balance and quality stop
fighting. The tax is just gone.
The paper stakes the claim in a single
line.
Trick two, multi-token prediction. A
normal language model is trained on one
thin instruction, given everything so
far, predict the very next token. One
token of supervision per position.
That's a surprisingly sparse signal. The
model sees a whole context and only ever
gets graded on the single next word.
Deep seek, V3 densifies it. Alongside
the main next token head, it bolts on a
small chain of extra modules, and each
one predicts a token further into the
future. Module one guesses token T plus
two, module two guesses T plus three,
and so on. So, now, at every single
position in the sequence, the model
isn't graded once. It's graded on the
next several tokens at the same time.
Same data, several times the
supervision. And the crucial design
choice is how these modules connect.
They're sequential, not parallel. Each
module is fed the hidden state of the
module before it. So, the prediction of
token T + 3 is conditioned on your best
guess of T + 2. The full causal chain
stays intact. You're never cheating by
predicting the future independently of
the tokens in between. That richer,
denser training signal is most of why
MTP makes the base model measurably
better. And then it pays a second
dividend at inference, which is the next
beat.
Let's open up one MTP module because the
wiring is clever. Every module shares
the main model's embedding table and
output head, so you're not paying for a
whole new vocabulary projection per
module, just one small transformer block
each. To predict at depth K, the module
glues together two things, the hidden
state from the previous depth and the
embedding of the actual future token
you're aiming at. It runs those through
its transformer block, and the shared
head turns the result into a
distribution over the token at position
T + K + 1. Stack a few and you've got a
little pipeline forecasting several
steps ahead. Each step honestly
conditioned on the last. Now, the
inference payoff. During training, these
modules sharpened the main model. At
generation, you repurpose them as a
speculative decoder. Let the cheap MTP
heads draft the next few tokens in one
shot. Then let the main model verify
them in parallel and keep the ones it
agrees with. DeepSeek reports the second
token guess is accepted around 85 to 90%
of the time, roughly 1.8 tokens out per
step instead of one. Nearly double the
decoding speed, free from modules you
trained anyway.
Trick three is the one nobody dared ship
at this scale, train in eight-bit
floating point. Normally, you train
these giants in 16-bits. Deep seek V3
runs the expensive matrix multiplies,
the big gems, in FP8, 8-bits per number.
That roughly halves memory and doubles
throughput on the tensor cores. The
catch is that 8-bits is a cruelly narrow
window. In the E4M3 format, you get
about 4-bits of exponent, tiny dynamic
range. So numbers a bit too big saturate
and numbers a bit too small flush to
zero. And real activations have
outliers. One freakishly large value in
a block. Pick one scale factor for that
whole block and the outlier forces the
scale so high that every normal value
underflows into noise. Naive FP8 quietly
destroys your gradients. The fix has two
parts. First, fine-grain scaling. Don't
scale a giant tensor with a single
number. Chop it into tiles and blocks of
128, each with its own scale, so every
little neighborhood sits in FP8 sweet
spot. Second, high precision
accumulation. The tensor cores multiply
in 8-bits, but the running sums get
promoted to full FP32 before adding. So
rounding errors don't snowball. Master
weights and optimizer state stay higher
precision, too. The payoff was a genuine
first. FP8 training proven stable on a
model this enormous.
Let's make the outlier problem concrete
because it's the whole reason tiles
exist. Say you're quantizing a block of
values that mostly sit around one,
ordinary numbers, but a single entry is
500. To fit FP8, you multiply the block
by a scale, so the biggest value lands
near the top of the range. But that
scale is set by the 500 and once you
apply it, your ordinary values around
one get divided down to about 0.002. And
in FP8's coarse spacing, that rounds to
zero. The outlier survived, everything
else got erased. One bad apple, whole
block ruined. Tile-wise scaling breaks
the coupling. Instead of one scale for
the block, cut it into small tiles,
strips of 128, and each tile gets its
own scale from its own local maximum.
The tile with the outlier scales around
500, the calm tiles next door scale
around one, and keep all their
precision. The damage is quarantined to
the tile that actually had the giant.
Then the second half, accumulation. A
matrix multiplies hundreds of these
eight-bit products, and adding many tiny
numbers in eight bits bleeds precision
fast. So, the instant products leave the
tensor core, they're accumulated in full
FP32. Cheap where you can be, precise
exactly where it counts.
Trick four is pure systems engineering,
and it's the quiet hero behind the cost
number. When your experts are spread
across 64 GPUs, every token has to be
physically shipped to whichever GPUs
hold its chosen experts, and the results
shipped back. That's an all-to-all,
everybody talking to everybody, and in
cross-node MoE, it's brutal. The compute
to communication ratio is roughly one to
one. So, for every second of math, you'd
spend a second just moving tokens across
the network. Do that naively, and half
your absurdly expensive cluster sits
idle, waiting on the wire. There's a
second waste, too. Pipeline parallelism
splits the layers across devices. Device
one runs the early layers, device two
the next, but at the start and end of
each batch, the later devices twiddle
their thumbs waiting for the earlier
ones. Those idle gaps are pipeline
bubbles. Dual pipe attacks both. It
feeds micro batches in from both ends of
the pipeline at once, two streams
flowing in opposite directions, which
fills the bubbles a one-directional
pipeline leaves empty. And within each
chunk, it overlaps the phases. While the
GPU grinds through the attention and
expert math for one micro batch, the
all-to-all traffic for another is flying
across the network underneath it, hidden
completely. Communication stops being a
line on the clock. It disappears behind
the compute, which is exactly what lets
them scale experts across nodes almost
for free.

A word on the iron, because the software
tricks only pay off if the hardware's
fed. DeepSeek V3 trained on a cluster of
2048 H800 GPUs, NVLink inside each node,
InfiniBand between nodes, with custom
all-to-all kernels written to squeeze
every drop of that bandwidth. They also
went to war on memory. They recompute
cheap things like RMS norm during the
backward pass instead of storing them.
Keep an exponential moving average of
the weights parked in CPU memory, and
notably, skip tensor parallelism
entirely, which is usually mandatory at
this scale, but eats bandwidth. Stack
the savings and the whole run came in at
2.79 million GPU hours start to finish
without a single roll back.
What did it eat and what happened after?
Pre-training ran on 14.8 trillion
tokens, a bigger, cleaner mix than V2,
weighted toward math and code, plus a
two-stage stretch that pushes the
context window out to 128,000 tokens.
Then came post-training, supervised
fine-tuning to teach it to follow
instructions, and reinforcement learning
to align it with what people actually
want. And here's the fun bit. They
distilled reasoning ability out of
DeepSeek, R1, their long
chain-of-thought model, and folded R1's
step-by-step problem-solving habits in a
V3, while carefully keeping its answers
from ballooning into rambling essays. A
frontier-based model, then polished with

Frontier-class results, startup-sized

Now, is any of this actually causal, or
just correlation with a big budget? The
paper runs the controlled experiments,
and two of them matter. First, the
load-balancing showdown. Train the same
model two ways. Once with the classic
auxiliary loss, once with the auxiliary
loss-free bias trick, and compare. The
bias-based version doesn't just balance
the experts as well. It also scores
better on validation. That's the key
result, and it's the whole thesis in one
chart. The old method was paying a
quality tax for its balance, and
removing the tax gives you both. You
genuinely don't have to choose. Second,
multi-token prediction. Switch it off,
and the benchmark scores drop across the
board on reasoning, on coding, on math.
The denser training signal isn't
cosmetic. It moves the numbers. They
also confirm the modules can be thrown
away at inference with zero cost to the
main model or kept around for the
speculative decoding speed up. So, each
trick earns its seat for a different
reason. The bias fixes a problem the old
fix created and MTP squeezes more model
out of the same tokens. Not a pile of
lucky hyper parameters, a set of choices
that each pay their own way.
Let's stack the whole recipe back up.
Start with the backbone, multi-headed
attention to keep inference memory small
and a deep seek and we mixture where
only a sliver of the experts fire per
token. Keep those experts balanced not
with a loss that fights your model, but
with a per expert bias nudge like a
thermostat after every step, plus or
minus a hair. So, load evens out and
quality never pays for it. Densify the
training signal with multi-token
prediction, extra sequential heads that
each forecast a token further ahead,
which sharpen the model and then double
as a speculative decoder at inference.
Cut the arithmetic cost in half by
training in eight-bit floats,
quarantining outliers with per tile
scaling and catching rounding with FP32
accumulation. And hide the network
entirely with dual pipe, feeding the
pipeline from both ends and overlapping
the all-to-all traffic underneath the
compute so no GPU sits idle. None of
these is a silver bullet. It's a dozen
careful engineering calls, each shaving
off a slice of cost stacked until
frontier quality fits a few million
dollars instead of a few hundred. That's
the whole magic trick and there wasn't
any magic, just discipline.
The one-line version, balance the
