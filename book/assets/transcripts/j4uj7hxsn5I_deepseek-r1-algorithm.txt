# deepseek-r1-algorithm
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j4uj7hxsn5I

Let's break down DeepSeek Math and the
reinforcement learning trick buried
inside it, group relative policy
optimization, GRPO, a way to train a
model to reason without ever building a
critic network. This one quietly grew up
to become the engine behind DeepSeek R1.
To teach a model math with reinforcement
learning, the recipe sounds simple. Let
it write an answer, reward it when it's
right, nudge it to do more of that. But
the standard method, PPO, hides a second
neural network inside it. Not the model
you care about, a whole extra one,
roughly the same size, whose only job is
to guess how good each step was. You
train it, you store it, you run it every
single update. Half your reinforcement
learning compute goes to a network you
throw away at the end. DeepSeek's
question was blunt. What if we just
delete it? Group relative policy
optimization keeps the policy, kills the
critic, and replaces its careful per
step guess with something almost
embarrassingly simple, a group of
answers grading themselves against their
own average. A 7 billion parameter model
trained this way hits 51.7%
on competition level math. No
calculators, no voting. And the exact
same algorithm later became the
reinforcement learning core of R1. So
over the next 20 minutes, we're going to
build GRPO the honest way. Start from
PPO, walk its objective term by term,
plug in real numbers, write out the
pseudo code, and watch an entire neural
network disappear.
Start from the ground floor. We have a
policy. That's just our language model
with weights theta. Hand it a math
question Q, and it samples an answer O
one token at a time. A reward function
scores that answer. One if the final
result is correct, zero if it isn't. Now
we want to reshape the model so it
produces more of the good answers. The
tool is the policy gradient. Push up the
probability of the tokens inside high
reward answers. Push down the rest. But
there's a catch that wrecks the naive
version, variance. If every correct
answer just gets a reward of one, the
gradient can't tell a brilliant solution
from a lucky guess. They both simply
look good and the learning signal
flashes around. The fix is to stop
asking was this good and start asking
was this better than expected. Subtract
a baseline, a reference level of reward
you'd have gotten anyway, and keep only
the surplus. That surplus has a name,
the advantage, written A, and basically
everything in PPO and GRPO is a single
fight over one question. Where does that
baseline come from?
PPO's answer starts with a clever safety
rail. We're updating theta right now,
but we sampled our answers from the
older weights, theta old, so the two
policies disagree and we correct for it
with an importance ratio, rho. The new
policy's probability of a token divided
by the old policy's probability of that
same token. If rho is bigger than one,
the new model likes that token more than
the old one did. Multiply rho by the
advantage and you have the raw
objective. Reinforce the tokens the new
model favors in proportion to how much
better than baseline they turned out.
But raw importance ratios can explode
and blow up training. So PPO clips. It
takes the minimum of two things, the
honest term, rho times A, and a clipped
term where rho is pinned between 1 minus
epsilon and 1 plus epsilon. In plain
words, you're allowed to move each
token's probability, but only so far in
a single update. Step past the fence and
the gradient flattens and stops pulling
you further. It's a trust region,
enforced with one little min simple,
stable, effective. There's just one
quietly expensive word sitting in the
middle of it, the advantage, AT, one
value per token. Where does PPO actually
get it?
From a second network, the critic, also
called the value model. PPO estimates
the advantage with generalized advantage
estimation, GAE, and GAE needs a
function V that predicts from a
half-finished answer how much total
reward to expect from here on. That
predictor is not free. In language model
RL, it's typically another transformer
about the same size as the policy. So,
now you're training two big models
locked together. Double the memory to
hold them. Double the forward and
backward passes, plus extra machinery to
keep the critic's targets from drifting.
And here's the cruel part. The critic's
job is nearly impossible in this
setting. The reward only arrives at the
very end, one number, right or wrong,
after hundreds of tokens. From that
single late signal, the value model is
somehow supposed to assign a sensible
score to every intermediate token.
Getting that per token estimate accurate
is genuinely hard. And when it's off, it
quietly poisons every advantage computed
downstream. So, PPO pays for a whole
extra network and still struggles to
train it well. That is exactly the tax
GRPO refuses to pay.
The authors name the problem directly.
Here's the move, and it's beautiful in
how little it needs. Instead of building
a network to guess the baseline, GRPO
just samples more answers. For a single
question Q, draw a whole group G
outputs, say four for our examples, or
in the real runs, 64. Score every one of
them with the reward model, giving a
little set of rewards, R1 through RG.
Now, the baseline writes itself. It
isn't a network's prediction anymore.
It's the group's own average reward.
Each answer's advantage is simply how
far it beat or missed that average. Land
above the group mean, you get a positive
advantage and every token in you is
reinforced. Land below, negative, pushed
down. And to keep the scales sane across
easy questions and brutally hard ones,
they also divide by the group's standard
deviation. So, the advantage of answer I
is RI minus the mean all over the
standard deviation. That's it. It's a
Z-score. The answers grade themselves on
a curve and every token in a given
answer inherits that one number as its
advantage. No value model, no GA, no
second transformer. The single most
expensive component in PPO just got
replaced by a mean and a standard
deviation.
Now, the full objective, built one piece
at a time, and notice it's really just
PPO wearing the group idea. Outermost
layer, average over the group, one over
G times the sum across all G answers.
That's the relative in group relative.
Every answer is judged inside its own
cohort. Next layer in, for each answer,
average over its tokens, one over the
answer's length times the sum over
positions T. Pure bookkeeping so far,
average over answers, then over tokens.
Now, sitting inside those sums is an
object you already know cold, the
clipped PPO surrogate. The importance
ratio row now indexed by answer I and
token T times the advantage. The minimum
of row times A hat and its clipped twin,
exactly the trust region from before.
The only thing that changed is what
feeds the advantage, not a critic's
estimate, but our group Z-score A hat
sub I shared by every token in answer I.
And then, tacked onto the very end, one
new term, minus beta times the KL
divergence between the current policy
and a frozen reference model. That's the
leash. It keeps the model from wandering
too far from where it started while it
chases reward. Same skeleton as PPO,
group baseline in the middle, KL on the
outside. That entire line is GRPO.
Let's make it real with actual numbers
because the whole thing turns on one
little calculation. One question, a
group of four answers. The reward model
scores them. 1.0 for a clean correct
solution, zero for a flatly wrong one,
0.8 for a mostly right attempt, and 0.2
for a mess that stumbled somewhere.
First, the baseline, the group mean. 1 +
0 + 0.8 + 0.2 all over 4 = 0.5. Then the
spread, the standard deviation of those
four rewards works out to about 0.42.
Now the advantages, each reward minus
the mean divided by that spread. Answer
one, 1 - 0.5 is 0.5 over 0.42 gives +
1.19. Answer two, 0 - 0.5 over 0.42 -
1.19. Answer three lands at + 0.71.
Answer four, - 0.71.
Now look at what happened. Answers one
and three came in above average,
positive advantage, so the gradient
pushes every token in them upward.
Answers two and four fell below,
negative advantage, push down. No
network judged anything. Four rewards, a
mean, and a standard deviation, and the
model already knows which answers to
become more like. That is the entire
advantage computation start to finish.
They stated in a single sentence.
Back to that KL leash because GRPO does
something subtly smart with it. In the
classic PPO recipe for language models,
the KL penalty gets folded into the
reward. At every token, you subtract
beta times the log ratio of policy to
reference and hand that adjusted reward
on to the advantage machinery. It works,
but it tangles the penalty up inside the
advantage estimate and by extension
inside the critic. GRPO pulls it back
out. The KL is not shaped into the
reward at all anymore. It's added
directly to the loss as its own clean
term, so it never touches the advantage.
And they estimate it with a particular
unbiased formula, the K3 estimator. Take
the ratio of reference probability to
policy probability, subtract the log of
that same ratio, then subtract one. That
little expression has two properties you
want. Its average equals the true KL
divergence. That's the unbiased part.
And unlike the naive log ratio, it is
guaranteed to be greater than or equal
to zero for every single sample. So, the
penalty is always a genuine pushback
toward the reference, never a negative
fluke that accidentally rewards drifting
away. Cleaner signal, lower variance,
and no entanglement with a critic you no
longer even have.
Let's write it as an actual loop because
it's shorter than you'd guess. Outer
loop over training iterations, set the
reference model equal to the current
policy. That's the frozen anchor the KL
term measures against. Inner loop over
steps, grab a batch of questions and
freeze the current weights as the old
policy, theta old, the one you'll sample
from. Now, for each question in the
batch, sample G outputs from that old
policy. Score all G of them with the
reward model. Compute the group's mean
and standard deviation and turn each
reward into its advantage, reward minus
mean over standard deviation, the one
number every token in that answer will
carry. Then take a few gradient steps
that maximize the GRPO objective, the
clipped importance ratio term weighted
by those advantages, minus beta times
the KL to the reference. Apply the
update, move to the next batch, and
optionally keep refreshing the reward
model as the policy gets stronger. Now,
scan the whole loop for a value network.
Sample, score, normalize, step. There
isn't one. Every place PPO would have
spun up a critic, GRPO just computes a
mean. That absence, that missing box, is
the entire contribution.
Put the two side by side and the whole
paper compresses into one swap. Both
algorithms share the identical skeleton.
Sample answers, form an advantage, feed
it into the same clipped surrogate, take
a trust region step. Everything is
shared except one box, how you get the
advantage. PPO's box is a second neural
network. Feed it a partial answer, it
predicts expected future reward. GA
turns that stream of predictions into a
per token advantage. Powerful and
general, and it cost you a whole extra
model to train, tune, and store. GRPO's
box is arithmetic. Sample a group,
subtract the mean, divide by the
standard deviation. That's the box. Same
trust region, same importance ratio,
same clip, a different baseline. And
because providing that baseline was the
only reason the critic existed in the
first place, deleting the critic doesn't
break anything downstream. The group
already supplies the reference level the
value network used to laboriously guess.
One box changed, and half the compute,
half the memory, and a big chunk of the
training instability walked out the door
with it.
One refinement worth knowing. So far
we've used outcome supervision, a single
reward at the very end, and every token
in the answer shares that same one
advantage. That's fine for short
problems, but a long proof might be
flawless for 20 steps and then blow it
on the 21st, and outcome supervision
blames the entire thing equally. So,
GRPO also supports process supervision.
A reward model scores each reasoning
step, not just the final answer. You
normalize all of those step rewards
across the group the same way, and each
token's advantage becomes the sum of the
normalized rewards from that step
onward. Now, credit lands where the good
reasoning actually happened, and blame
lands on the step that broke, while the
group relative baseline carries over
completely unchanged.
And there's a longer game, too. A reward
model is only really trustworthy near
the kinds of answers it was trained on.
But, as the policy improves, it starts
producing solutions the reward model has
never quite seen, and its scores begin
to drift. So, GRPO can be run
iteratively. Every so often, you take
the policy's fresh samples, use them to
keep training the reward model, mixing
in a slice of older data with a replay
buffer, so it doesn't forget what it
already knew. Then, you reset the
reference model to the current policy,
and continue. The reward model, the
reference, and the policy all advance
together in rounds, instead of trusting
one frozen judge for the entire run.
That's GRPO, rebuilt from the ground up,
critic deleted, and the math still
checks out. Quick breath. If watching a

So, where does a 7 billion model get
world-class math skill? Not from the
algorithm, from the data, and the way
they mined it is a small masterpiece of
bootstrapping. The open web is full of
mathematics, drowning in noise. Step
one, grab a seed of known good math
pages. They used OpenWebMath, and train
a fast, cheap classifier, fast text, to
recognize this reads like math. Step
two, sweep that classifier across common
crawl, 40 billion deduplicated web
pages, and recall everything that scores
high. Step three, rank by score, and
keep only the top slice. Now the clever
part, the loop. Look at which web
domains the classifier keeps pulling
from. Have humans annotate the genuinely
mathematical URL patterns on those
domains. Fold the newly found pages back
into the seed corpus, and retrain the
classifier sharper than before. Each
round recalls the math that the previous
round missed. After four iterations,
they'd pulled 120 billion tokens of real
mathematics out of the web, several
times larger than any math corpus before
it. And then, crucially, decontaminate.
Strip out anything that overlaps the
benchmarks, so the test scores are
actually earned, not quietly memorized.
The paper closes on a genuinely
clarifying idea. Take all the ways we
fine-tune language models. Plain
supervised fine-tuning, rejection
sampling fine-tuning, its online
variant, DPO, PPO, and GRPO. And up
front, they look like a zoo of unrelated
tricks. But write down each one's
gradient, and they all share a single
template, a source of training data, and
a coefficient sitting out front that
says how hard to push each example up or
down. That coefficient is the only thing
that genuinely differs between them.
Supervised tuning trusts every example
equally. The RL methods weight each one
by a reward-based advantage. Seen
through this lens, GRPO isn't an ad hoc
hack at all. It's one principal choice
of gradient coefficient, with the groups
serving as its baseline.
And the payoff is real. The base model,
before any of this reinforcement
learning, already scored 36% on
competition math, beating a 540 billion
parameter model more than 70 times its
size. Then instruction tuning, and then
GRPO, and the numbers climb again. Grade
school GSM8K goes from 82.9 up to 88.2%
and competition level math from 46.8 to
51.7. The first open model over 50, no
calculators, no majority voting. And
GRPO matches or beats PPO on these tasks
while training a single network instead
of two. Cheaper and better at the same
time, which is a combination you almost
never actually get.
For the record,
it's worth asking which ingredient

Second, the RL step. Start from the

GRPO lifts every benchmark, including

So this story isn't one magic trick.
It's a great corpus that builds the raw
ability and a cheap, stable RL method
that reliably squeezes more reasoning
back out of it. Each piece earns its
place in the pipeline.
