# deepseek-r1-full-recipe
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y6uhwBF3F7k

Let's break down the core idea behind
this paper in depth. DeepSeek R1,
incentivizing reasoning in large
language models via reinforcement
learning from DeepSeek.
Here's something that should not work.
You take a raw language model, no work
solutions, no human written reasoning,
nobody showing it how to think, and you
do exactly one thing. Every time it gets
a hard math problem right, you pay it.
That's the entire training signal. Get
the answer correct, get rewarded. And
over thousands of steps, with no one
teaching it, the model starts thinking.
It writes longer. It checks its own
work. It backtracks, tries another
route, catches its own mistakes. On the
hardest competition math in the room,
its score climbs from barely one in six
to beating the average human competitor,
landing in the same league as OpenAI's
O1. Reasoning wasn't installed. It grew
out of a reward. Over the next 20
minutes, we're going to rebuild this
whole thing twice. First, R1-0,
pure reinforcement learning straight
onto the base model, and the emergent
reasoning that stunned everyone. Then
the four-stage pipeline that took that
feral genius and turned it into
something you'd actually ship. And
finally, how they bottled it into models
small enough to run at home. Let's go.
First, what even is a reasoning model?
An ordinary model blurts the answer
straight out, one forward pass, done. A
reasoning model does something more
human. Before it commits, it thinks out
loud. It writes a long chain of thought,
scratch work, dead ends, sanity checks,
and only then gives its final answer.
And here's the useful part. The longer
it thinks, the more of these hard
problems it gets right. Thinking is just
compute spent at answer time instead of
training time. So, the question this
whole paper asks is simple. Where does
that thinking come from? Do you have to
teach it? Or can it grow on its own from
nothing but a nudge in the right
direction?
So, how do you reward being right
without a human grading every single
answer? Two pieces. First, the
optimizer, GRPO, the same trick
introduced in the DeepSeek Math paper.
Quick refresher. For one question, you
don't grade a single answer. You sample
a whole group of them, say 16. You score
each, and then each answer's advantage
is dead simple. How far above or below
the group's own average did it land?
Beat your peers, get pushed up. Drag
behind, get pushed down. The group
grades itself. No separate critic
network, no value model to train
alongside, which is exactly what makes
it cheap enough to run at this scale.
Second, the grader itself, and it's
almost insultingly simple. Two rules.
Accuracy, is the final answer actually
correct? For math, check it against the
known solution. For code, run it against
the test cases. And format, did you wrap
your thinking inside the think tags?
That's the whole reward. No neural
network judging quality, because the
moment you let a learned model hand out
the rewards, the policy learns to fool
it. Reward hacking. Dumb, rigid rules
can't be gamed.
Now, the bit that made this paper
famous. Everyone else builds a reasoning
model like this. First, supervised
fine-tuning. Feed it a mountain of
human-written, step-by-step solutions,
and make it copy them. Then, maybe some
reinforcement learning to polish.
DeepSeek's move with our 1-0 was to
delete the entire first stage. No
supervised fine-tuning. No human
reasoning examples. Nothing. You take
DeepSeek V3 base, raw off the
pre-training line, and you point GRPO
straight at it. Here's the loop, and it
never changes. Hand the model a hard
problem. Let it generate a group of full
attempts, thinking tags and all. Grade
each with those two rigid rules, right
answer, correct format. Compute the
group relative advantage. Nudge the
weights toward whatever the winners did
and away from the losers. Repeat 10,000
times. Notice what is never in that
loop, a single example of good
reasoning. Nobody ever shows it a
well-reasoned solution. The only
pressure on the entire system is land
the answer more often. The bet is that
good reasoning is simply whatever gets
you there. And that if you reward the
destination hard enough, the model will
discover the road entirely on its own.
Insane bet. It worked.
So, what actually happens as you run
that loop? Two curves start climbing
together, and they are the heart of the
paper. The first is accuracy. On AIME,
the brutal American high school
Olympiad, the model starts at 15.6%
barely one problem in six. As training
grinds on, it climbs and climbs and
lands at 77.9%
pass at one. And if you let it vote
across several tries, 86.7. That's from
mostly failing to comfortably beating
the average human competitor with not
one line of reasoning ever handed to it.
The second curve is the tell for why.
It's the average response length. How
many tokens the model spends thinking
before it answers. And nobody told it to
think longer. But that curve rises right
alongside accuracy, hundreds of tokens,
then thousands. The model discovers
entirely on its own that spending more
thought gets the answer right more
often. So, the reward quietly pushes it
to think more. That's the
self-evolution. Two curves, accuracy and
thinking time, dragging each other
upward, driven by nothing but a reward
for being correct.
And then there's the moment that gave
this paper its legend. Somewhere in the
middle of training, on a gnarly algebra
problem, our 1-0 is grinding through the
usual way. Square both sides, rearrange,
push the symbols around, and then
mid-solution, it does this. It stops. It
writes, "Wait, wait, wait." That's an
aha moment I can flag here. And it goes
back, reevaluates its own steps,
realizes it was heading somewhere wrong,
and restarts down a better path. Read
that again because of what it isn't.
Nobody wrote a check-your-work
instruction. There is no rule in the
reward for self- correction. No example
of back-tracking in the training data.
There is no training data. The model
discovered, purely because catching
mistakes leads to right answers more
often, that it pays to stop and doubt
itself. An emergent metacognitive move
in a strangely human little voice that
fell out of a reward signal. The authors
basically stopped and stared. They call
it an aha moment for them, too. This is
the beat to sit with. Reflection wasn't
programmed. It was incentivized into
existence.
And the authors flag it, too. The moment
their model learned, on its own, to
rethink.
So, our 1-0 is a phenomenal reasoner. It
is also, bluntly, a terrible product.
Here's the catch with rewarding only the
final answer. You constrain the
destination, but you left the road
completely unconstrained. And the model
took that freedom and got weird. Its
chains of thought are a mess to read,
sprawling, disorganized, optimized for
landing the answer, not for a human
following along. Worse, because the base
model was trained heavily on both
English and Chinese, our 1-0 starts
language mixing. It'll reason halfway
through a problem in English, slide into
Chinese for a few lines, then slide
back. To the reward function, that's
totally fine. The answer's still right,
so it never gets penalized. To a human
trying to actually use it, it's
unreadable. This is the fundamental
tension of pure RL. The reward measured
whether it was correct and nothing else.
So, everything the reward didn't
measure, like being readable, quietly
rotted. Great brain, bad manners.
So, how do you keep the emergent
brilliance but fix the manners? You
don't patch our 1-0. You rebuild in four
stages, and this pipeline is the real
deep seek R1. Stage one, cold start.
Before any RL, you gently warm the base
model on a few thousand hand-curated
examples of long, readable,
human-friendly reasoning. Not enough to
teach it how to think, just enough to
fix the voice and give it a clean format
to speak in. Stage two, reasoning RL.
Now, you run the same GRPO loop we just
watched, rediscovering deep reasoning.
But you bolt on one new reward, a
language consistency bonus that pays the
model for keeping its whole chain of
thought in a single language. That kills
the language mixing directly. Stage
three, rejection sampling then
supervised fine-tuning. Take that
polished RL model, sample from it a lot,
keep only the outputs that are actually
correct. That harvest becomes roughly
600,000 clean reasoning examples, plus
around 200,000 general ones, writing,
facts, everyday questions. And you
fine-tune a fresh base on all of it.
Stage four, a final RL pass across every
kind of prompt, tuning for helpfulness
and harmlessness. Four stages, each
feeding the next. Same emergent
reasoning, finally presentable.
Let's make sure each stage earns its
place because the ordering is the clever
part. Why cold start first instead of
just running RL like zero did? Because
our 1-0 proved RL can find a reasoning,
but also that RL alone finds it in an
ugly, unreadable form. Cold start plants
a small, clean seed of readable format.
So, when RL explores, it explores from a
well-behaved starting point instead of a
feral one. Why rejection sampling in the
middle? This is the sneaky smart move.
The stage two RL model reasons
beautifully, but is hard to steer. So,
instead of shipping it, you use it as a
data factory. Generate mountains of
solutions, keep only the correct ones,
and you've quietly converted a hard to
control RL policy into clean supervised
data you fully own. And you fold in
general skills, writing, facts, that
reasoning RL never touched. And why RL
yet again at the very end? Because after
all that fine-tuning, you want the final
alignment. Helpful, harmless, coherent
across every kind of question, not just
math. Each stage exists to repair
exactly what the stage before it
couldn't.
Here's that recipe in their own words.
It begins with a pinch of cold start
data.
If watching reasoning bootstrap itself
out of a reward is your kind of magic,
like, subscribe, and send this to the
friend who still insists you need a
million labeled examples for everything.
Because the next part is the sneaky
clever bit. Can we take this giant brain
and shrink it down to something you can
actually run?
Here's the problem. The full R1 is a 670
billion parameter mixture of experts
monster. Almost nobody can run it. So,
DeepSeek did the generous, obvious in
hindsight thing. Remember, R1 now
produces gorgeous, long, self-checked
reasoning traces. So, treat those traces
as a textbook. You take a small,
ordinary dense model, a Quinn or a
Llama, anywhere from 1 and 1/2 billion
parameters up to 70, and you simply
fine-tune it. Plain supervised learning
on 800,000 of R1's own outputs. No
reinforcement learning on the small
model at all. Just, here is how the big
model thought, copy it. And the small
models drink it in. They inherit the
reasoning, the long chains, the
backtracking, the self-checks from
watching the giant. The numbers are
startling. The distilled 14 billion
model out-reasons baselines several
times its size. And the distilled 32
billion hits 72.6 on AIME, out-thinking
models many times bigger and clearing
OpenAI's 01 mini. A tiny open model
reasoning like a giant because a giant
taught it.
Now, the finding that flips the whole
story, and it's the one worth
remembering. Obvious next question, why
distill? Why not just do to the small
model exactly what we did to the big
one? Point pure reinforcement learning
straight at it and let it discover
reasoning itself, our 1-0 style. So,
they tried it. They took a 32 billion
base model and ran the full, expensive,
large-scale RL on it directly. And it
mostly stalled. It crawled up to roughly
the level of existing 32B reasoning
models and no further. Then they took
that same 32B and just distilled our
ones traces into it instead. It blew the
RL version away across every single
benchmark. Two bars, one lesson. For a
small model, discovering reasoning from
scratch with RL is brutally expensive
and caps out low. But copying the
reasoning a bigger model already
discovered is cheap and lands far
higher. Discovery is expensive. Copying
discovery is nearly free. So, if you
want a small reasoner, don't grow one,
inherit one.
So, where does it all land? The full
Deep Seek R1 goes toe-to-toe with
OpenAI's 01217,
the frontier closed reasoning model at
the time, on the hard math, on math 500,
on competition coding, on graduate level
science. Roughly 79.8 on AIME, 97 plus
on math, 500, and it outranks 96% of
humans on Codeforces. And the distilled
models are state-of-the-art for their
size, top to bottom. But the kicker, the
thing that made this a genuine moment in
the field, is that all of it shipped
open. The weights, the recipe, the whole
distilled family, out for anyone to
download. Frontier reasoning, no longer
behind a wall.
And on the math, the paper says it flat
out, on par with OpenAI's a 11217.
Let's isolate what every piece actually
bought, because that's where the real
lessons hide. R1-0 versus full R1. The
pure RL0 model is the stronger raw
discoverer. It's the one that grew
reasoning from literally nothing. But
it's unreadable, and it mixes languages.
The four-stage pipeline barely trades
away any reasoning power, and in
exchange it buys readability, single
language consistency, and general
helpfulness. That's the deal the entire
paper is built on. A hair of raw
reasoning for a model humans can
actually use. Then the small model axis,
distillation versus direct RL.
Distilling R1's traces into a small
model wins decisively. Running RL
directly on that same small model stalls
out. So the two knobs pull in opposite
directions depending on scale. At the
frontier, with a huge base and huge
compute, RL is how brand new reasoning
gets discovered in the first place. Down
at small scale, you don't rediscover it,
you inherit it. Each result quietly
tells you which tool fits which size.
So here's the whole thing, start to
finish. One, the reward is dumb on
purpose. Just check the final answer and
the format, no neural greater to hack.
Two, optimize it with GRPO, a group of
tries, each graded against its own
average, no critic. Three, point that
straight at the raw base model, no
supervised warm-up, and reasoning
emerges on its own. Longer thinking,
self-checking, the aha moment and all.
Four, wrap that feral brain in a
four-stage pipeline. Cold start for a
readable voice, reasoning RL to sharpen
it, and fix the language mixing.
Rejection sampling into clean data, then
a final RL pass to align it. And five,
distill those hard-won reasoning traces
down into small models anyone can run.
Reasoning grown from a reward, then
bottled and handed out.
The one-line version, don't teach a
model to reason. Pay it to be right.
Reward the correct answer, optimize with
group relative reinforcement learning,
and the chain of thought grows itself.
Then copy that thinking into something
small enough to run anywhere.
And it leaves a genuinely strange
thought. If reasoning, reflection,
self-doubt, the aha moment was just
sitting in there, waiting for the right
reward to coax it out. What else is in
these models right now, dormant, waiting
for the right reward to show up? That's
my read. Tell me yours. Until next time.
