Let's break down flash attention. Fast
and memory efficient exact attention
with IO awareness from Tri Dao and
collaborators at Stanford. Same math as
ordinary attention. Radically less
memory and a lot faster.
Here is the attention matrix. Every
token scoring every other token. For a
thousand token sequence, that's a
million numbers. Standard attention
writes that whole grid out to memory,
reads it back, writes it again. Flash
attention computes the exact same answer
and never builds the grid at all. Same
outputs to the last bit, but linear
memory and up to seven and a half times
faster. The trick isn't a cleverer
approximation. It's being careful about
where the numbers live.
Let's ground ourselves in exactly what
attention computes because every symbol
here comes back. You have three
matrices, queries, keys, and values.
Each n tokens tall by d dimensions wide,
where d is the head dimension, usually
64 or 128. Multiply q by k transpose and
you get the score matrix s, an n by n
grid where entry ij is how strongly
query i attends to key j. In practice,
you also scale it by 1 over root d to
keep the numbers tame. Softmax each row
of s, turning those scores into a
probability distribution, the weights p.
Then multiply p by v to blend the values
into the output o, again n by d. Two
matrix multiplies with a softmax in the
middle. That is all attention is. And
the entire performance story lives in
that one object in the center, the n by
n grid. One more thing worth holding on
to. This whole computation happens per
attention head and per sequence in the
batch independently. So in practice,
you're running many of these little n by
n problems at once, which is exactly why
the memory cost piles up so fast.
Here's the part most courses skip. A GPU
is not one uniform pool of memory. It's
a hierarchy. At the bottom, HBM,
high-bandwidth memory, 40 GB on an A100.
Fast by everyday standards, but slow by
the GPU's zone, about 1 and 1/2 TB a
second. Above it, SRAM, on-chip memory
sitting right next to the compute units.
Screaming fast at 19 TB a second, but
tiny, megabytes, not gigabytes. Now,
watch what standard attention does. It
computes the scores and writes the whole
n by n matrix out to HBM. Reads it back
to run softmax. Writes the result. Reads
it again to multiply by V. The floating
point work is trivial. The time is spent
shuttling that giant matrix in and out
of slow memory. That is the wall.
And that is the paper's central claim.
To see why that matters, you have to
know operations come in two flavors.
Compute-bound, the clock is set by the
arithmetic. A big matrix multiply with a
fat inner dimension is the classic case.
There's so much math that the memory
reads barely register. And memory-bound,
the arithmetic is trivial, and you're
purely limited by how fast bytes move.
The telltale is arithmetic intensity,
flops per byte. Softmax, dropout,
masking, every element-wise pass over
that n by n grid has almost no math per
byte, so they're deeply memory-bound.
Put real numbers on it. Standard
attention on GPT-2
does about 67 gigaflops of arithmetic,
but moves 40 GB through HBM and takes 42
milliseconds. Almost all of it just
waiting on memory. The matmuls aren't
the problem. The grid is.
So, the obvious question, why write the
grid out at all? Why not compute the
output directly and skip it? Here's the
obstacle that stops the naive approach.
Softmax over a row needs the entire row
to normalize even a single entry. You
need the maximum across all in scores
for numerical stability and the sum of
all in exponentials for the denominator.
Both are reductions. They couple every
column in the row together. You can't
finalize any one weight until you've
seen all of them. That coupling is
exactly why the standard implementation
materializes the full row of scores
first and why everyone assumed you had
to. Break that coupling, compute the
row's softmax without ever holding the
whole row, and the entire algorithm
falls out.
So, let's look at softmax carefully
because the fix hides inside it. You
never exponentiate raw scores. A score
of a few hundred overflows the instant
you hit e to the x. So, the numerically
stable softmax first finds the row's
maximum, m, and subtracts it. Then it
exponentiates each shifted score, fi
equals e to the xi minus m. It sums
those into l and finally divides f by l.
Subtracting the max changes nothing in
the answer. It cancels in the ratio, but
it keeps every exponential between zero
and one. Now, notice what actually
carries the information. A whole row
softmax is summarized by just two
running numbers, the max m and the sum
l. Track those two as data streams in
and you have everything.
Here's the key idea and it's genuinely
elegant. You don't need the row all at
once. You can process it in chunks and
keep the two statistics running. Say the
row arrives in two blocks. Process the
first, local max m1, local sum l1. Now
the second block arrives and it contains
a larger value, a new maximum. Problem.
L1 was computed by subtracting the old,
smaller max, so it's on the wrong scale.
The fix is a single multiplication. You
rescale the old sum by e to the m1 minus
the new max, a factor below one that
shrinks it to exactly the scale it would
have had if you'd known the true maximum
from the start. Then add the second
block sum, update the max, and you're
ready for the next block. Column by
column, block by block, you reconstruct
the exact softmax denominator, never
holding more than one block at a time.
The authors put it in a single line.
Let's make that exactness because it's
the part people distrust. Take a full
row, six scores, and stream it in three
blocks. After block one, the running max
is three, the running sum is 1.135.
Block two brings a four, a bigger max,
so we rescale and add. The sum becomes
1.467.
Block three brings a five, rescale
again, and we land at 1.590.
Now compute the softmax the ordinary
way, all six scores at once. The
denominator is 1.590
to the digit, identical. And here's the
storage detail you'll want when you
implement it. Instead of keeping the max
and the sum separately, fold them into
one number, l equals m plus log l, the
log sum exp. That single value per row
is everything the backward pass will
ever need.
But the softmax denominator was never
the real goal, the output o is. So push
the exact same trick one level up onto
the output itself. Alongside the running
max and running sum, keep a running
output vector. When a new block arrives
with a new maximum, rescale that running
output by the very same correction
factor. Then add this block's
contribution, its local softmax weights
p tilde times its values v. You divide
by the running sum only once at the very
end. This is the line that makes the
whole paper work, so sit with it. The
output is assembled block by block, and
every time a bigger score appears,
everything accumulated so far gets
rescaled in place. You never hold the
full row of attention weights, never
build a piece of that n by n matrix,
just a single d-dimensional vector
corrected as you go. And why is
deferring that final division safe?
Because the unnormalized output and the
running sum scale together. The
correction factor multiplies both, so
one division at the very end recovers
exactly the right weighted average.
Keeping everything measured against the
running max also means no intermediate
value ever overflows.
Now, translate that onto real hardware
where the blocks aren't abstract.
They're chosen to fit SRAM. You can't
load the n by n grid, but you can load
small tiles. Cut the queries into row
blocks and the keys and values into
column blocks. Each pairing of a Q block
with a K block carves out one small tile
of the score grid, BR rows by BC
columns. The block sizes are picked so
that a K and V block, a Q block, and the
running accumulators all fit on chip at
the same time. So, the enormous n by n
grid becomes a checkerboard of little
tiles, and at any instant the hardware
holds exactly one of them in fast
memory.
Where do those block sizes actually come
from? Count what has to live on chip at
once. A block of Q, a block of K, a
block of V, and the output block O. Four
things, each about BC by D. They all
have to fit in SRAM of size M. So, 4 *
BC * D has to stay under M, which pins
the block size to M over 4D. And within
that ceiling, bigger is better. The
larger each block, the fewer times you
sweep over all of Q, and the fewer trips
you make to HBM. So, run time drops as
the block grows, then flattens once your
bandwidth free, or you simply run out of
SRAM. That's the whole tuning knob, one
number set by the hardware.
Now assemble the full forward pass.
There are two nested loops. The outer
loop walks across the column blocks of K
and V, loading each pair from HBM into
SRAM one time. The inner loop walks down
the row blocks of Q. For each tile,
entirely on chip, compute the scores, Q
block times K block transpose. Take the
tiles row max, exponentiate, row sum,
its local statistics. Merge those into
that query blocks running max and
running sum using the correction factor
from before. Then update the query
blocks output in place. Rescale what's
there, add this tiles weighted values.
Write back just the output block and its
two little statistics and move to the
next tile. Because K and V are the outer
loop, each query blocks output gets
revisited and refined as new key blocks
arrive. And that loop order is
deliberate. K and V are what you reload
into SRAM. So keeping a pair fixed while
you stream all the query blocks past
them minimizes how often the large
matrices have to cross the memory bus.
When both loops finish, O is exact, bit
for bit what standard attention would
give, and the N by N matrix never once
touched HBM.

One implementation detail ties it
together. In standard attention each
step is its own GPU kernel. Matmul,
write to HBM, softmax, write to HBM,
matmul, write to HBM. Three separate

Now let's actually count the memory

becomes a theorem. Standard attention
touches HBM on the order of n squared.
It writes and reads that whole grid

Multiply it out and you get n squared d

More math, a fraction of the shuttling,

Training needs gradients and the

refused to store. Awkward. The answer is

use it, throw it away. This looks like

Recomputing a tile in fast SRAM is so

And the gradients themselves are cleaner
than you'd fear, just the chain rule
done by hand. The gradient of V is P
transpose times DO. The gradient of the
weights, DP, is DO times V transpose.
For the scores, softmax has a known
Jacobian, diagonal of P minus PP
transpose, which gives DS equals P
element-wise times the quantity DP minus
D. And D is the trick that keeps
everything linear. Instead of forming a
full N by N product for each row, it
collapses to a single scalar, the row
sum of DO element-wise O, which is just
DOI dotted with OI. One number per row.
With DS in hand, the input gradients
drop right out. DQ is DS times K and DK
is DS transpose times Q. Every one of
these is a matmul over tiles, no N by N
array anywhere.
And here's the backward pass as a loop,
the same shape as the forward run in
reverse. Outer loop over the K and V
blocks, inner loop over Q. First,
recompute this tile's probabilities, the
scores from Q and K, then P as the

stored L. That one line stands in for
the entire N by N matrix we chose not to
save. Then accumulate. The value
gradient gets P transpose times DO,
compute DP, and that cheap little D, the
row sum of DO times O, form DS. And push
out the input gradients. DQ gets DS
times K, DK gets DS transpose times Q.
Each block's gradients accumulate across
the loop, everything stays linear in
memory, and you get exact gradients
