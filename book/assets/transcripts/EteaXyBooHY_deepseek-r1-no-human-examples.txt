# deepseek-r1-no-human-examples
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EteaXyBooHY

Let's break down the core idea behind
this paper, DeepSeek, R1 from DeepSeek.
Nobody taught this model to think. They
just paid it to be right, and it taught
itself to reason. No worked examples, no
human solutions, just a reward for the
correct answer, and a model that slowly,
on its own, started thinking longer,
checking itself, and doubling its score
on the hardest math in the room.
Here's how everyone else taught
reasoning. You collect a mountain of
human-written step-by-step solutions,
and you make the model copy them,
supervised fine-tuning. It works, but
it's expensive, it's slow, and it
quietly caps the model at human
reasoning. It can only imitate the paths
we already wrote down.
DeepSeek made a bet. Throw that whole
stage out. Start from the raw base
model, skip the human demonstrations
entirely, and just let reinforcement
learning loose. Try an answer, get
graded, do more of what worked. That's
it. They call this one R1-0,
zero human reasoning data.
But reinforcement learning usually needs
a second network, a critic, guessing how
good each step is. Expensive. GRPO
throws that out, too. For one question,
sample a whole group of answers, score
them, then each answer's advantage is
dead simple. How far above or below the
group's own average did it land? Beat
the pack, get pushed up, drag behind,
get pushed down. The group grades
itself, no critic required.
And the grader is almost insultingly
simple. Two rules. One, is the final
answer actually correct? Check it
against the real solution. Two, did you
put your thinking inside the tags?
That's the entire reward. Notice what's
missing. Nothing tells the model how to
reason. We only score whether it landed
the answer.
And they're blunt about it. The grade is
nothing but whether the final answer is
right.
So, what does a model do when the only
thing rewarded is being right? It starts
thinking longer. With zero instruction
to do so, its answers stretch from a
couple lines to hundreds, then thousands
of tokens. It begins to backtrack, try
another route, double-check itself.
Reasoning wasn't programmed in. It grew
because thinking longer got the answer
right more often.
And then, mid-solution, it did this, the
model catching itself.
The scoreboard backs it up.
One problem, our 1-0 is a genius that
mumbles. Unreadable, switching between
English and Chinese mid-thought. So, the
full R1 wraps that raw reasoning in a
four-step polish. A pinch of clean
example thoughts to fix the voice, then
the same reasoning RL, then keep only
its best answers and fine-tune on them.
Then a final RL pass for everything,
safety, writing, the works. Same
emergent brain, now presentable, and it
ties OpenAI's 01 at 79.8.
If watching a model teach itself to
think is your kind of magic, like,
subscribe, and send this to the friend
who still thinks you need a million
labeled examples for everything. Stick
around because the next part is the
sneaky clever bit.
R1 is a 600 billion parameter monster.
Most people can't run it. So, here's the
gift. Take R1's answers, those long,
self-checked reasoning traces, and just
fine-tune a small, ordinary model on
them. Plain copying. A 7, 14, 32 billion
model drinks in R1's thinking. The
distilled 32B hits 72.6 on AIME,
out-reasoning models many times its
size.
And here's the punchline that flips the
whole story. They also tried the other
way. Run that same expensive
reinforcement learning directly on the
small model and let it discover
reasoning from scratch. It barely
budged. Turns out it's far better to let
one giant model do the hard exploring,
then hand the finished thoughts to the
small one. Discovery is expensive,
copying discovery is cheap.
So, the whole recipe, start from the
base model, reward only the right
answer, score each try against its group
with GRPO, no critic, and reasoning
emerges on its own, aha moment and all.
Polish it in stages, so it's readable,
then distill those thoughts down into
models you can actually run.
The one-line version, don't teach a
model to reason, incentivize it. Reward
correct answers, optimize with group
relative reinforcement learning, and
watch the chain of thought grow itself.
Then copy that thinking into something
small.
It leaves a genuinely strange thought.
If all it took was rewarding the right
answer, how much reasoning was hiding in
these models the whole time? Just
waiting for a reason to show up. That's
my read. Tell me yours. Until next time.
