# deepseek-v4-1m-context
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5mL7qxQ9GLw

Let's break down the core idea behind
this paper, DeepSeek V4, towards highly
efficient million-token context
intelligence from DeepSeek AI.
A million tokens. That's a stack of
context taller than most entire novels
handed to a model all at once. Every
file in a code base, a year of chat
history, a pile of research papers. The
dream of an AI that reasons over that
much at once keeps smashing into the
same wall. Attention gets
catastrophically expensive as the
context grows. DeepSeek V4's whole
mission is to knock that wall down, to
make a million-token context not a
heroic one-off, but something you run
routinely. And it pulls it off while
using a small fraction of the compute
and memory its predecessor needed.
Here's the wall. In a normal
transformer, every token has to attend
to every other token. So, the cost grows
with the square of the context length.
Double the context, quadruple the work.
At a million tokens, that's
astronomical. And there's a second cost,
the KV cache, the model's running memory
of every past token, keeps growing,
eating gigabytes of GPU memory just to
remember what it's already seen.
Together, those two are what make long
context a luxury. But long context is
exactly what the frontier now needs.
Reasoning models that think for pages,
agents running for hours, analysis
across whole document sets. So, the
question is, how do you keep a million
tokens in mind without paying a million
tokens worth of cost?
DeepSeek V4's answer is to stop treating
all that memory as equally precious. The
KV cache is where every past token is
stored for attention to look at. So, if
you shrink and prune what's in there,
everything downstream gets cheaper. V4
uses a hybrid of two compressed
attentions in different layers. The
first is compressed sparse attention. It
does two things at once. It squeezes the
KV cache down along the sequence,
compressing runs of tokens into leaner
summaries, and then it attends sparsely,
letting each new token look at only a
selected handful of the most relevant
past chunks instead of all million.
Compress the memory, then read only the
parts that matter. The second flavor is
heavily compressed attention. This one
compresses the KV cache far more
aggressively. A tiny dense summary of
the whole past, but then it attends to
all of it because now there's so little
to attend to. One keeps detail but reads
sparsely. The other reads everything but
keeps almost nothing. Stack both kinds
through the network and you get the best
of each. The model still sees the whole
million token sweep, but the compute and
the memory collapse to a sliver of what
full attention would demand.
The second upgrade is quieter, but
clever. It rebuilds the residual
connection. Every deep network leans on
residuals, the little shortcut that lets
a layer add its update on top of the
input, so signals and gradients flow
cleanly through hundreds of layers. V4
replaces that single shortcut with
hyperconnections. Instead of one stream
running through the network, several
parallel streams, with the layers
learning how to mix between them. A
richer set of highways for information
than a single lane. The catch with
giving a network that much freedom is
that it can wander off and destabilize
training. So they keep the mixing
manifold constrained, mathematically
reined in, so the streams stay
well-behaved. Stronger information flow
without the instability that usually
comes with it.
The third upgrade is the optimizer, the
algorithm that actually turns gradients
into weight updates. For years almost
everyone has used Adam and its cousins.
V4 switches to a newer optimizer called
Muon, which conditions those updates
more carefully, and in practice
converges faster and trains more stably
at this scale. Faster convergence means
fewer steps to reach the same quality
and on a model with over a trillion
parameters trained on 32 trillion
tokens, that's a very large amount of
compute and money saved. A small change
to the update rule, a big change to the
bill.
The efficiency engine, in their words.
If making a million token context
actually affordable is the kind of
engineering you're here for, like,

Put it together and the long context
numbers are the headline. At a full
million token context, DeepSeek V4 Pro
runs each token for just 27% of the
compute its predecessor needed and holds
its memory in 10% of the KV cache. The
lighter flash model pushes that to a
tenth of the compute and 7% of the
memory. That's the difference between a
million token context being a stunt and
being routine and it doesn't trade away
smarts to get there. In its maximum
reasoning mode, it sets a new bar among
open models and goes toe-to-toe with the
frontier closed models, Gemini, GPT,
Claude on reasoning, coding and agentic
tasks while being the one that can
actually afford to think across a
million tokens.
The efficiency win.
So, the recipe for affordable million
token context. Attention's quadratic
cost and a bloated KV cache are what
make long context expensive. So, shrink
what attention has to look at. Blend two
compressed attentions. One that squeezes
the memory and then reads only the
relevant chunks and one that squeezes
the memory even harder and reads all of
the little that's left. Upgrade the
plain residual into constrained hyper
connections for richer, stable
information flow. Swap in the Muon
optimizer for faster, steadier training.
The result, frontier-level reasoning
that keeps a million tokens in mind for
a fraction of the cost.
The one-line version, DeepSeek V4 makes
a million token context routine by
compressing and pruning what attention
has to remember, so it can think across
a novel's worth of tokens at a fraction
of the compute and memory.
The theme across these DeepSeek papers
is the same, and I love it. The frontier
isn't only about training a bigger
brain. It's about refusing to pay full
price for the obvious approach. Full
attention over a million tokens is the
obvious approach, and it's ruinous. So,
they asked which parts of the past
actually deserve full attention, and the
answer turned out to be, not many.
