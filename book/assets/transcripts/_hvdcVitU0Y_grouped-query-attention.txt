# grouped-query-attention
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_hvdcVitU0Y

Let's break down the core idea behind
this paper, GQA from Google Research,
the one-line change to attention that
made serving these models affordable.
Eight times less memory during
generation and almost no quality lost.
That's the entire paper and it's why
nearly every model you've run this year
quietly uses it. Here's what nobody
tells you about serving a large model.
The weights aren't the problem. The
thing eating your GPU is a giant,
ever-growing scratch pad called the KV
cache. GQA shrinks it with a change
small enough to fit in one sentence.
Start with generation. Every time the
model writes a token, it attends to
every token before it. Recomputing all
those keys and values each step would be
madness, so we cache them. Write once,
reuse forever. Except the cache grows
with every new token and each step the
GPU reads the whole thing back just to
produce one word. So decoding isn't
compute bound, it's bandwidth bound.
Your very expensive matrix multipliers
sit idle waiting on memory.
How big does it get? For every past
token you store a key and a value for
every head in every layer for every
sequence in the batch. So it scales with
context length, with batch size and with
head count. At long context and real
batch sizes the cache stops being a
rounding error. It dwarfs the weights
and it's fat for one reason. Every head
insists on its own private keys and
values.
Noam Shazeer's fix for years earlier was
gloriously blunt. What if all the query
heads shared one set of keys and values?
Keep the query heads, they still ask
different questions, but hand them all
the same key and value. That's
multi-query attention and it shrinks the
cache by the number of heads. 64 heads,
64 times smaller. Except you've just
deleted a big chunk of the model's
capacity. Quality slips and training
gets twitchy, loss spikes, and models
that diverge when you fine-tune on long
inputs.
So, GQA asked the question everybody
skipped. Why is this a light switch?
Sort the query heads into groups. Every
head inside a group shares one key head
and one value head, but each group gets
its own. Eight groups instead of 64
heads. The cache shrinks eightfold and
every group still keeps its own view of
the past. Now, the ends of that dial.
One group per head is exactly multi-head
attention. One group total is exactly
multi-query. GQA isn't a new mechanism.
It's the slider that was always sitting
between them.
Here's the part that made this actually
happen. A new attention layout normally
means training from scratch. Nobody's
doing that for a checkpoint they already
shipped. So, instead, inside each group,
average the key projections together and
average the value projections. Mean
pooling literally. The shared head
starts life as the consensus of the
heads it replaces. Already roughly right
and better than keeping one head and
binning the rest or starting from noise.
Then, keep pre-training on the same data
for 5% of the original steps.
5% and they checked exactly where the
returns run out.
If average a few weights and have your
serving bill is your kind of trick, like
subscribe and send this to whoever pays
for your GPUs.
So, where do you set the dial? The trade
is beautifully lopsided. Going from one
group to eight costs almost no speed.
The cache is already small enough that
bandwidth stops being what you wait on.
Keep climbing toward one group per head
and the cost bites hard. Quality moves
the other way. Most of what multi-query
gave up comes back in the first few
groups. So, the sweet spot isn't a
compromise. It's a corner. Eight groups
and you're done.
The receipts on T5 XXL full multi-head
scores 47.2
at 1.51% sample. Multi-query is six
times faster, but slips to 46.6.
GQA with eight groups, 0.28 multi-query
speed and 47.1 shoulder to shoulder with
full multi-head, which is why GQA became
the default. Llama 2, Llama 3, Mistral,
most of what you serve. It's also the
ancestor of DeepSeek's multi-head latent
attention, which we covered in the
DeepSeek series. Same target, different
move. Compress the cache instead of
sharing it.
And here's the row that made everyone
switch.
So, the whole thing in one pass.
Generation is bottlenecked by rereading
the KV cache and the cache is fat
because every head hoards its own keys
and values. Multi-query shares one set
across all of them. Fast, but it costs
quality and stability. GQA splits the
difference. Group the heads, one
key-value pair per group. A clean dial
from multi-head to multi-query. Convert
an existing checkpoint by mean pooling
inside each group, up-train 5% and you
land at multi-query speed with
multi-head quality.
One line. Don't give every attention
head its own keys and values and don't
force them all to share one. Group them
and the cache shrinks by the size of the
group while the model barely notices.
Which leaves a question I keep chewing
on. GQA works because attention heads
are redundant enough to share and nobody
really knows how much sharing is too
much. Every architecture since has been
quietly probing that edge. That's my
read. Tell me yours. Until next time.
