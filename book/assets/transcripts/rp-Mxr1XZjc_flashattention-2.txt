# flashattention-2
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rp-Mxr1XZjc

Let's break down Flash Attention 2,
faster attention with better parallelism
and work partitioning from Tri Dao at
Princeton and Stanford. Same math, seven
times faster.
You already know what attention does.
Here's the part nobody mentions. The
slow bit isn't the math. This is the
score matrix. Every token against every
other token. And standard attention
builds the whole thing, writes it to
memory, reads it back, writes it again.
The arithmetic is trivial. The shuttling
is everything. Flash Attention 2
computes the exact same numbers, not an
approximation, the same numbers, and
never builds the grid at all. Seven
times faster than the attention most
people were running, and the reason long
context stopped being a luxury.
A GPU isn't one pool of memory. It's a
hierarchy, and the gap is brutal. At the
bottom, HBM, 80 GB on an A100, about 2
TB a second. Sounds quick. Up top, SRAM,
on chip, right beside the compute units,
19 TB a second, but you get roughly 200
KB of it per multiprocessor. So, the
only question that matters is what lives
up there. Standard attention's answer is
nothing. It parks the N by N grid in HBM
and drags it across the bus three
separate times.
Which makes attention memory bound, and
that's the whole insight. Operations
come in two flavors, compute bound, a
fat matrix multiply, so much arithmetic
the memory traffic vanishes into the
noise. Memory bound, barely any math per
byte, so you sit there waiting on the
bus. Softmax, masking, dropout, every
element-wise pass over that grid is
deeply memory bound. So, cutting flops,
the optimization everyone reaches for,
optimizes the thing that wasn't costing
you anything. Count bytes moved, not
operations performed.
So, why not skip the grid and go
straight to the output? Because SoftMax
is nosy. To normalize one score, you
need the maximum over the entire row for
stability, and the sum of every
exponential in it. Both are reductions
across all in columns. You can't finish
one weight until you've seen all of
them. Here's the fix. Take the row in
chunks and carry two running numbers,
the largest score so far and the running
sum. When a chunk arrives with a bigger
maximum, you rescale the old sum by one
exponential factor, then add. Exact at
every step. Nothing approximated.
Now, put it on silicon. Cut the queries
into row blocks, the keys and values
into column blocks, and the grid becomes
a checkerboard of tiles small enough to
fit in SRAM. Load one tile, compute its
scores on chip, fold them into that
query blocks running statistics. And
here's the move. Keep the running output
vector, too, and rescale it by the same
correction factor whenever a bigger
score turns up. One fused kernel, load,
compute, write. The N-by-N matrix is
never allocated. Quadratic memory
becomes linear, and the answer is
bit-for-bit identical.
The whole recipe in one sentence.
Training needs gradients, and the
textbook backward pass wants exactly the
matrices we refuse to store. Awkward.
The answer is recomputation. The forward
pass kept the output and one number per
row, the log sum exp, and that's enough
to rebuild any tile from scratch in fast
memory on demand. It looks like
checkpointing, trade compute for memory.
But attention is memory-bound, so
recomputing a tile in SRAM beats
fetching one from HBM. You save the
memory, and you get faster.
If winning by counting bytes instead of
flops is your idea of a good time, like,

because version two gets properly petty.
Here's the thing though, flash attention
was fast and still only hit 25%
of what the chip can do while a plain
matrix multiply gets 80 or 90. Version
two hunts that gap. First target,
non-matmal flops. An A100 does 312
teraflops of matmal on its tensor cores,
but only 19 and a half of ordinary math.
So, each non-matmal operation costs 16
times more. So, pull the division out of
the inner loop, accumulate the output on
scaled, divide once at the end, and
store one log sum exp instead of two
statistics. Identical output, far fewer
expensive instructions.
Second target, keeping the GPU busy.
Version one gave each attention head its
own thread block. Fine when batch times
heads clears 108 the 100's
multiprocessor count. But, long context
means small batches and half the chip
goes idle. So, version two parallelizes
over sequence length two. Flip the loops
so query sit outside and every block of
rows becomes an independent worker that
talks to nobody. Inside a block, version
one split the keys across warps forcing
them to swap partials through shared
memory. Version two splits the queries
instead. Each warp owns its rows end to
end.
Add it up, about two times faster than
flash attention across the board, 1.7 to
3 depending on head dimension and
masking, and up to 10 times a standard
PyTorch implementation. 230 teraflops on
an A100, 73% of theoretical peak. That's
matrix multiply territory for something
with a softmax in the middle of it. End
to end, GPT models train at 72% model
flops utilization. Translated, 16,000
token context for the price of eight.
For the record.
So, the recipe. Attention is memory
bound. Count bytes, not flops. Never
materialize the N by N grid. Stream the
soft max with a running max and a
running sum, so it stays exact. Tile
into SRAM and fuse it into one kernel.
Recompute in the backward pass instead
of storing. Then version two. Spend your
cycles on matmuls. Split the work across
sequence length. Give every warp its own
rows. Same attention, several times the
speed.
One line. Attention was never compute
bound. Count trips to memory instead of
flops. Keep the whole computation on
chip and the exact same math runs
several times faster, which is what made
long context affordable.
Which leaves an uncomfortable question.
Flash attention two didn't change the
math by a single symbol. It just moved
the numbers to a better neighborhood.
So, how much of what we call a better
algorithm is really just better memory?
That's my read. Tell me yours. Until
next time.
