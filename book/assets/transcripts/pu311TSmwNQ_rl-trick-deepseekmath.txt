# rl-trick-deepseekmath
# video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pu311TSmwNQ

Let's break down the core idea behind
this paper, DeepSeek Math, and the
reinforcement learning trick that
quietly powers DeepSeek's reasoning
models.
To make a model better at math, you
reward it when it gets the answer right
and nudge it to do more of that. Simple.
Except the standard recipe secretly runs
a second neural network, just as big as
the first, whose only job is to guess
how good each step was. DeepSeek asked,
"What if we just delete it?" A 7 billion
model, and it hits 52% on competition
math.
First, the fuel. Great math reasoning
needs great math data, and the open web
is full of it, buried in noise. So, they
build a little filter, hand it a seed of
known math pages, train a fast
classifier to recognize, "This looks
like math." Sweep Common Crawl, keep the
best, and repeat. Round after round, it
pulls 120 billion tokens of real
mathematics out of the internet.
Now, the reinforcement learning. The
usual method, PPO, has a problem hiding
in plain sight. To know whether an
answer was better than expected, it
trains a second model, the critic, to
predict a baseline score for every
token. That critic is about the same
size as the model you actually care
about. Double the memory, double the
compute, and it's finicky to train when
the only reward comes at the very end.
Here's DeepSeek's fix, and it's
beautifully cheap. Skip the critic
entirely. For one question, sample a
whole group of answers, say eight. Score
them all. Now, the baseline isn't a
network's guess, it's just the group's
own average. Each answer's advantage is
simply how far above or below the group
mean it landed. Beat the average, get
reinforced, fall below, get pushed down.
The group grades itself on a curve, and
the second network is gone.
They say it plainly.
If RL tricks that delete half the
computer your thing, like, subscribe,
and send it to the friend still training
a critic network.
And it works. That 7 billion base model
already scores 51% on the math
benchmark. Open source territory that
used to belong to giants. Then GRPO
pushes it further. Grade school math
jumps to 88% competition math past 50.
Same model, cheaper RL, better
reasoning.
In their words,
the recipe, mine a mountain of math from
the open web, then reinforce reasoning
without a critic, sample a group, let
their average be the baseline, and push
the above average answers up. That's
GRPO, and it's the engine that would
later drive our one.
One line, you don't need a critic
network to judge an answer. Just sample
a group and grade against their average.
Cheaper RL, sharper reasoning.
Which is a funny idea to sit with. The
model improves by comparing itself to
other versions of itself. How far does
self-competition alone get you? That's
my read. Tell me yours. Until next time.
